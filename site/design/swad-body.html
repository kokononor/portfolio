Swad: How a Momo Café That Couldn't Make Rent Found Its Regulars

In December 2025, Swad served one or two customers a day and lost ₹10,000 a month. It sits on a quiet residential lane in Ranchi, where nobody wanders past by accident. Three months later it was serving twenty a day, eight in ten were coming back, and it cleared ₹20,000 in profit.

Hero photo: Swad storefront

The street didn't change. What changed was what Swad promised its customers, and whether it kept that promise every day.

01

In short

Swad didn't have an awareness problem. It had a trust problem.

People noticed the café, walked in, and still left without ordering, because they couldn't count on it being open, fresh, or the same as last time.

So I stopped trying to out-stock a market of 50 shops. I cut the menu to nine items built around momos, rebuilt every recipe so it didn't need a skilled cook, and fixed the basics customers had stopped believing in. The result was a ₹30,000 swing in monthly profit, which pays back the ₹2 lakh relaunch in about seven months.

Where Swad stood

Three facts shaped every decision that followed.

01

Nobody discovers Swad.

It's on a residential lane, not a market street. The only people who pass are neighbours, and they pass every day. A café like this lives or dies on whether those same people come back.

02

Good cooks don't stay.

Ranchi's café kitchens hire from a small pool with high turnover. Any plan that depended on one talented cook would collapse the week that cook left.

03

The money was real.

The café belongs to my family. The relaunch had a fixed budget of ₹2,00,000 and no investor behind it, so every rupee had to come back out of the till.

What the footfall showed

The funnel holds two separate problems. Most people who noticed Swad never came in, because from the street it didn't look like a café. But the bigger loss came after that. People who had already walked in, seen the menu and looked around still left without ordering. Signage couldn't fix that. Something inside was telling them not to trust the place.

05

Choosing one bet

With ₹2 lakh and a two-person core team, I could afford one real bet. These were the options.

No

Grow the menu to match the market

Would fix: The 30 people who couldn't find what they wanted

More dishes means more stock sitting around, which hurts freshness, and more skill in the kitchen, which hurts consistency. It would answer one complaint by making two others worse, and Swad would still be outnumbered by 50 shops.

No

Discounts and offers

Would fix: Getting more people to try it

The first problem was trust, not price. Discounts would bring in one-time bargain hunters and break the food-cost limit.

Not on its own

Refresh the storefront and interior only

Would fix: People noticing Swad but not walking in

It fixes the smaller leak and leaves taste, freshness and hours exactly as they were.

The bet

Narrow the menu and make it reliable

Would fix: People leaving without ordering, stale food, erratic hours, repeat visits

A small menu built on the dish Ranchi already buys most, made reliable through systems instead of skilled staff, with a storefront that finally says what Swad is.

The trade-off I had to defend

Thirty people told me Swad didn't have what they wanted, and my answer was to cut the menu from more than 30 items to nine. On paper, that looks like ignoring them.

Mostly they wanted choice, not a particular dish Swad was missing.

So I went back and asked what they meant. Five styles of momo, running from mild to properly hot, give a group of four friends something each without adding a single new ingredient to the store room. I accepted that people who wanted a full multi-cuisine menu would keep going to the market, which serves them better anyway.

The bet held after launch. Customers turned out to want something they couldn't get anywhere else more than they wanted the same plate every stall already sold.

What we built

A menu that decides for you

Momos were already the best-selling food in the area, so I made them Swad’s whole identity. Five regional styles and four drinks, with the five styles on a single line from mild to hot.

A first-timer can choose in two seconds instead of reading through 30 items. And a regular always has a next step, “try the one hotter”, so they can explore without going anywhere else.

Five spice blends of my own

When every stall nearby sells a similar ₹80 plate, a fifth momo stall needs a reason to exist. I created a spice blend for each style. None of them is a packet masala or a copy of a regional recipe.

IMAGE PLACEHOLDER

The five blends

The five spice blends in labelled jars, with a measuring spoon.

Fig. 6 Each blend is mixed in batches and measured by the spoon.

The blends carry most of the strategy. They answer the interview requests for “something spicier” and “something new” without adding a dish to the menu. They’re mixed in batches and measured by the spoon, so the flavour is fixed at prep time, not by whoever is at the stove. And a rival can copy a menu or a price in a week. Five original blends take far longer to copy.

Cost shaped the recipes too. The first Lucknow gravy used cashews and cream and cost ₹60 a plate, 40% of a ₹149 price. Soaked seeds and well-whisked curd kept the flavour and brought the cost down to ₹24, or 16%.

Getting them right took repetition. Lucknow and Hyderabadi needed around 80 test batches between them, and Delhi took 30. Jharkhandi came quickly because it’s the food I grew up with, and Sikkim stays close to the traditional taste.

The tasters were neighbours, family, friends and old customers. I dropped ideas drawn from Tamil Nadu and Maharashtra cooking: I’ve never been to either place, and I didn’t want to fake an authenticity I couldn’t vouch for.

The number I missed

Conversion rose sixfold or more, but it fell short of my target, and I think the reason matters. Once the trust problem was fixed, price was the main thing still stopping people. Some walk-ins still compare Swad’s plate with the market’s and leave.

I chose not to chase them. Average order value doubled, and that is what turned the loss into a profit. Discounting to win back bargain hunters would have broken the food-cost guardrail and brought back the customer mix Swad had just moved away from.

How I measured repeat visits

The 80% is an estimate, and I’d rather say so. Most regulars pre-order by phone, and on a lane where people know one another, the owners can tell a returning caller from a new one. I counted repeat callers against all callers. It’s directional, not precise.

What’s next

I held delivery back on purpose. Food that’s inconsistent gets punished harder in app ratings than at the counter. Now that the kitchen holds steady through staff changes, Swad is launching on Swiggy and Zomato and starting to build a brand online.

08

Looking back

WHAT I’D DO DIFFERENTLY

01

Measure from day one.

I’d log every order against a phone number or UPI handle from the first day. My repeat rate is a careful estimate. Tracked data would have made it a fact, and would have shown which fix moved which number.

02

Stagger the launch.

Shipping the hours and freshness fixes two weeks ahead of the new menu and interior would have shown how much reliability alone did for conversion. It was a free experiment, and I skipped it.

03

Go back to the 30.

I’d follow up with the people who said Swad didn’t have what they wanted and ask whether five styles of momo answered them. I believe it did, but I didn’t check.

WHAT IT TAUGHT ME

The product is everything the customer meets.

The storefront, the menu and the binder of procedures in the kitchen were one problem seen at three distances. If I’d fixed only what people could see, I’d have improved the first impression and left the real failure in place.

Deciding what you won’t be is a strategy.

The most valuable call in this project was what Swad would not offer: not variety, not discounts, and not delivery until the food was ready for it. Every no made consistency easier.

A constraint is a requirement.

The hiring market wasn’t a risk to note in a slide. It was a spec. Designing recipes that don’t need a skilled cook is why the results lasted beyond launch month.
